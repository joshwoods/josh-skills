# Source Evaluation Reference

Use this note when checking credibility, teaching research hygiene, or deciding whether a source belongs in a research brief.

This note is an example layer for the broader concepts in `research-concepts.md`, especially source-role matching, context recovery, claim tracing, and confidence calibration. Teach those concepts first; use the named sources here as concrete illustrations.

## Source Cards

### Purdue OWL: Evaluating Sources Of Information

- Institution: Purdue Online Writing Lab, Purdue University
- Source type: University writing and research guide
- URL: https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/conducting_research/evaluating_sources_of_information/index.html
- Date: The public page footer is current to 2026; individual guide pages do not expose a single publication date.
- Credibility: Purdue OWL is a long-running university writing and citation resource. The guide breaks evaluation into source type, author, audience, evidence, digital context, cross-checking, and timeliness.
- Useful sections: "Where to Begin," "General Guidelines," "Evaluating Digital Sources," and "Evaluation During Reading."
- Limitations: It is a general education guide, not a domain-specific evidence standard. It teaches screening and appraisal habits, not advanced systematic-review methods or statistical validity checks.

Core substance:

- Determine the genre of the source before judging it. Scholarly sources, popular sources, primary sources, and secondary sources serve different research jobs.
- Match the source type to the question. A cancer-treatment efficacy question usually needs scholarly/clinical evidence; a question about media portrayal may need popular or social sources as primary artifacts.
- Investigate the author: credentials, expertise, prior publications, institutional affiliation, publisher, and organizational backing.
- Determine the audience and purpose. Tone, vocabulary, assumed knowledge, and style reveal whether the source was made for specialists, general readers, advocacy, sales, or entertainment.
- Separate fact, opinion, and propaganda. Opinion can be useful if reasons and opposing views are represented; propaganda uses selective framing, emotional appeals, or true facts arranged to mislead.
- Check evidence quality: whether claims are sourced, whether coverage is comprehensive, whether the source oversimplifies, and whether references lead to better material.
- Cross-check specific dates, numbers, definitions, and claims against independent sources.
- Evaluate timeliness in context. Scientific and legal topics often require current sources; historical questions may depend on older primary documents.
- Digital-source checks must go beyond domain suffixes. `.gov` and `.edu` have access restrictions, while `.com`, `.org`, and `.net` are not inherently reliable or unreliable.
- Search results are shaped by ranking systems and SEO. A high search result is not proof of authority.
- Wikipedia can be useful for orientation and source discovery, but should not normally be treated as the final authority.
- Social media can be a primary source when the post itself is the object of study, but identity, affiliation, original context, and corroboration still matter.
- Online databases often contain more screened material than open web search, but database inclusion is not the same as correctness.

### Mike Caulfield: SIFT, The Four Moves

- Author: Mike Caulfield
- Source type: Web literacy method / digital source-evaluation essay
- URL: https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/
- Date: June 19, 2019
- Credibility: Caulfield is a prominent digital literacy educator; the method is widely taught in media literacy and library instruction contexts. The page is openly licensed under CC BY 4.0.
- Limitations: SIFT is optimized for fast web-context reconstruction. It is not enough by itself for systematic reviews, formal legal analysis, medical certainty, or statistical appraisal.

Core substance:

- Stop: pause before reading, believing, citing, or sharing. Ask whether you know the source, claim, and task well enough to proceed.
- Investigate the source: learn who produced the content, what expertise they have, what incentives or agenda they may carry, and what reputation they have before giving the content attention.
- Find better coverage: when the claim matters more than the particular article, leave the source and look for stronger coverage, consensus, disagreement, or primary material.
- Trace claims, quotes, and media to original context: locate the original paper, data, transcript, filing, image, video, or statement so you can see what was changed, omitted, or framed.
- Recontextualization is the point. Web content often strips away origin, incentive, consensus, and full context; SIFT puts those back before interpretation.
- Depth should match purpose. Quick checks are appropriate for casual web use; deep research should verify individual claims and original evidence.

## Practical Method

Use Purdue OWL for structured source appraisal and SIFT for fast web triage.

1. Identify the research job.
   - Is the source evidence for the claim, context about the topic, a primary artifact, or just a lead?
2. Classify the source.
   - Primary, secondary, scholarly, popular, data, official record, advocacy, marketing, commentary, social post, or unknown.
3. Run SIFT before deep reading.
   - Stop, investigate source, find better coverage, trace original context.
4. Run detailed appraisal before citing.
   - Author, audience, purpose, evidence, language, cross-checking, timeliness, references, conflicts.
5. Decide the allowed use.
   - Strong evidence, background only, lead only, artifact only, counterexample, or exclude.

## SIFT Checklist For Codex

- Stop:
  - What is the user's decision or learning goal?
  - Am I checking a source, a claim, a quote, a statistic, an image/video, or a general topic?
  - Would relying on this source create legal, financial, medical, reputational, or strategic risk?
- Investigate the source:
  - Who made it?
  - What do they know?
  - What do they want?
  - What is their track record?
  - Are they speaking from direct knowledge, expertise, interpretation, or promotion?
- Find better coverage:
  - Is there a primary source?
  - Is there a higher-authority source?
  - Is there independent reporting or expert consensus?
  - Are there credible dissenting accounts?
- Trace original context:
  - What is the original paper, filing, transcript, dataset, repo, statute, standard, image, or video?
  - Was the claim quoted accurately?
  - Was the date, jurisdiction, population, denominator, or comparison changed?
  - What was omitted?

## Detailed Appraisal Checklist

- Source type:
  - Primary: direct record or artifact.
  - Secondary: analysis, synthesis, interpretation, reporting.
  - Tertiary: encyclopedia, overview, index, generic explainer.
- Authority:
  - Named author or institution.
  - Relevant credentials or demonstrated expertise.
  - Publisher, journal, agency, court, standards body, company, or repository.
- Evidence:
  - Direct citations, methodology, data, references, code, appendix, or reproducible record.
  - Specific claims separated from interpretation.
  - Denominators, sample sizes, dates, geography, and definitions present.
- Purpose and incentives:
  - Inform, sell, persuade, advocate, entertain, recruit, raise funds, influence policy, defend reputation.
  - Funding, sponsorship, affiliations, conflicts.
- Language and framing:
  - Objective, cautious, proportional language.
  - Emotional claims, urgency, loaded adjectives, one-sidedness, missing caveats.
- Timeliness:
  - Current enough for the domain.
  - Publication date, update date, event date, dataset vintage.
- Corroboration:
  - Independent sources agree.
  - Primary source confirms.
  - Disagreement is mapped rather than ignored.
- Usability:
  - Strong enough to support a finding.
  - Useful only as background.
  - Useful only as a lead to trace.
  - Exclude due to credibility, relevance, or uncertainty.

## Examples

### Social Post

Research job: usually a lead or primary artifact, rarely final evidence.

1. Stop before accepting the screenshot or post text.
2. Identify the account owner, affiliation, expertise, and whether the account is authentic.
3. Search for better coverage from primary sources, subject-matter experts, or reputable reporting.
4. Trace embedded claims to original data, document, transcript, or media.
5. If the post itself matters, cite it as the artifact and clearly separate "the post says" from "the claim is true."

Decision rule: use a social post as evidence that someone said something, not as evidence that the thing said is true unless independently verified.

### News Article

Research job: context, discovery, timeline, named-source reporting, or lead to primary records.

1. Identify the outlet, reporter, beat expertise, publication date, corrections, and sourcing.
2. Extract each factual claim separately.
3. Trace claims to court filings, official statements, papers, filings, datasets, or interviews where possible.
4. Check whether other high-quality outlets report the same facts independently.
5. Note anonymous sourcing, conflicts, editorial framing, and missing voices.

Decision rule: reputable news can support event chronology and public statements, but primary documents should support legal, financial, technical, or quantitative claims.

### Company Claim

Research job: primary artifact for what the company claims; weaker evidence for whether the claim is true.

1. Identify whether the source is marketing copy, docs, investor relations, support, security disclosure, benchmark, or regulated filing.
2. Find incentives: sales, fundraising, litigation, regulatory compliance, recruitment, reputation.
3. Trace performance, adoption, cost, security, and compliance claims to filings, audited statements, changelogs, benchmarks, docs, or independent tests.
4. Compare against competitors, customers, regulators, and independent technical analysis.
5. Treat vague superlatives as unsupported unless operationalized.

Decision rule: company material is strong evidence for positioning and official commitments; it needs corroboration for comparative superiority or real-world performance.

### Academic Claim

Research job: evidence, method, or scholarly context.

1. Identify publication venue, peer-review status, author expertise, funders, conflicts, and date.
2. Read title, abstract, intro, methods, results, limitations, and citations before relying on the conclusion.
3. Check sample size, population, measurement, comparison, statistics, and whether conclusions exceed data.
4. Look for replication, meta-analysis, later citations, critiques, corrections, and retractions.
5. Distinguish paper-level evidence from field-level consensus.

Decision rule: a single paper is rarely enough for a broad conclusion; use it as one piece in a mapped evidence base.

## Educational Explanation

Good research starts before reading deeply. The first task is to recover context: who created the source, what kind of source it is, why it was created, what evidence it provides, and whether better evidence exists. A polished article, viral post, or official-looking domain can still be weak evidence. A rough primary artifact can be strong evidence for a narrow fact. The researcher's job is to match the source to the claim and the decision.

Use SIFT to avoid wasting attention on weak or decontextualized sources. Use Purdue-style appraisal to decide what role a source can safely play in the final answer. The output should not say "this source exists"; it should say what the source can and cannot prove.

## Output Pattern

When applying this note, include a compact credibility statement:

```markdown
Source quality: Medium. This is a reputable trade article with named sources and a clear publication date, useful for timeline and stakeholder quotes. It is not primary evidence for the technical performance claim, so I traced that claim to the vendor benchmark and two independent tests before using it.
```
