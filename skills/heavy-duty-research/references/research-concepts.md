# Research Concepts

Use this note to teach or apply the transferable ideas behind heavy-duty research. Named frameworks and sources in the other reference files are examples of these concepts, not the point of the skill.

## Concept Map

| Concept | Core Question | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Claim decomposition | What exactly is being claimed? | Prevents vague questions from producing vague evidence |
| Source-role matching | What job can this source safely do? | Stops weak sources from carrying strong conclusions |
| Context recovery | Who made this, why, for whom, and with what evidence? | Restores the context that summaries, posts, and search results strip away |
| Claim tracing | Where did this number, quote, image, or assertion originate? | Finds primary evidence and detects distortion |
| Evidence fit | Does the evidence match the population, time, geography, comparator, and outcome? | Avoids overgeneralizing true but irrelevant evidence |
| Literature graph navigation | What does this source cite, who cites it, and what sits nearby? | Builds a field map instead of a pile of links |
| Reading-depth control | How deeply does this source deserve to be read? | Saves attention for sources that affect the conclusion |
| Search transparency | Where did we look, and what did we exclude? | Makes the research auditable and easier to improve |
| Structured synthesis | What pattern emerges across sources? | Converts notes into decision-useful findings |
| Confidence calibration | How likely is this finding to survive better evidence? | Keeps the answer honest and useful |
| Transferable teaching | What should the user learn next? | Builds user capability beyond the immediate answer |

## Core Concepts

### Claim Decomposition

Break broad claims into testable parts before searching.

Ask:

- What entity, population, product, law, market, paper, or behavior is involved?
- What outcome is being claimed?
- What comparator or baseline is implied?
- What date, jurisdiction, version, or setting matters?
- Is the claim factual, causal, predictive, normative, or interpretive?

Example:

`"This tool is better for research"` becomes:

- Better for whom?
- Better on speed, recall, precision, cost, transparency, synthesis quality, or learning?
- Compared to what?
- On what tasks and date?
- Measured by whose evidence?

### Source-Role Matching

A source is not simply good or bad. It has a permissible role.

Common roles:

- Primary evidence: directly records the event, rule, data, quote, code, filing, or artifact.
- Measurement evidence: provides data or method that estimates the answer.
- Interpretive evidence: explains, analyzes, or critiques primary material.
- Discovery lead: points toward better sources but should not support a conclusion alone.
- Context source: helps understand vocabulary, history, or stakeholder views.
- Artifact: is itself the thing being studied, such as a social post, ad, changelog, or speech.
- Example source: illustrates a method or concept but is not evidence for the topic.

Rule: assign the source's role before using it in the answer.

### Context Recovery

Before trusting a source, recover missing context.

Ask:

- Creator: who made it?
- Capability: what do they know?
- Incentive: what do they want?
- Audience: who was it made for?
- Evidence: what support is provided?
- Timing: when was it produced and updated?
- Position: is it primary, secondary, or commentary?
- Omission: what would a skeptical reader want to see but does not?

This concept generalizes source-evaluation checklists and web-literacy methods.

### Claim Tracing

Trace important claims backward until you reach the strongest available origin.

Trace:

- Quotes to transcripts, recordings, filings, or original interviews.
- Numbers to datasets, tables, methodology notes, or filings.
- Screenshots to live pages, archives, or source media.
- Product claims to docs, benchmarks, changelogs, source code, or independent tests.
- Legal claims to statute, regulation, agency guidance, docket, or opinion.
- Scientific claims to paper, preprint, dataset, protocol, or review.

Stop when you reach a primary source or when the remaining gap is explicit.

### Evidence Fit

Strong evidence for the wrong question is weak evidence for this question.

Check fit across:

- Population or user segment.
- Geography or jurisdiction.
- Time period and version.
- Intervention, tool, policy, or exposure.
- Comparator or baseline.
- Outcome definition.
- Measurement method.
- Decision context.

Downgrade confidence when fit is indirect.

### Literature Graph Navigation

Treat scholarship and technical writing as a graph.

Move:

- Backward through references to foundations and methods.
- Forward through citations to updates, replications, criticisms, and extensions.
- Sideways through related work, same authors, same venues, same datasets, and adjacent terms.

Goal: identify the anchor sources, live debates, and consensus boundary.

### Reading-Depth Control

Not every source deserves the same depth.

Use depth levels:

- Scan: title, date, author, abstract/summary, headings, conclusion, references.
- Read: claims, method, data, limitations, and relevance.
- Audit: assumptions, calculations, primary evidence, citations, contradictions, and reproducibility.

Deep-read only sources that could change a finding, confidence level, or recommendation.

### Search Transparency

Record the research path well enough that another agent could continue it.

Track:

- Search date.
- Queries.
- Databases and source types.
- Inclusion criteria.
- Exclusion criteria.
- Sources included.
- Sources rejected and why.
- Known gaps.

This does not require a formal systematic review for every task; scale the record to the stakes.

### Structured Synthesis

Synthesis is not a summary stack. It is a comparison across evidence.

Group evidence by:

- Claim.
- Source role.
- Method.
- Population or segment.
- Time period.
- Jurisdiction.
- Agreement or disagreement.
- Bias or incentive.

Then answer:

- What is well-supported?
- What is plausible but under-evidenced?
- What is contradicted?
- What is unknown?
- What would change the answer?

### Confidence Calibration

Confidence belongs to findings, not sources.

Increase confidence when:

- Direct primary evidence answers the question.
- Independent sources converge.
- Methods are transparent.
- Evidence fits the user's context.
- Bias is low or unlikely to explain the result.

Decrease confidence when:

- Evidence is indirect, stale, sparse, or inconsistent.
- The source has strong incentives.
- The method is unclear.
- The sample is too small or unrepresentative.
- Important counterevidence is missing.

### Transferable Teaching

When the user wants educational references, teach the concept behind the work.

Good teaching references should:

- Explain fundamentals clearly.
- Provide durable vocabulary.
- Show how practitioners reason.
- Include canonical sources or primary docs when appropriate.
- Help the user inspect claims independently next time.

Avoid making the method itself the lesson unless the user asked to learn research methodology.

## Applying Concepts In Output

Use concept names sparingly in final answers. The user should see the benefits as clear scope, strong sources, transparent caveats, and useful educational references.

Use explicit concept language when it helps:

```markdown
I treated the founder's post as an artifact and discovery lead, not as evidence that the market claim is true. I traced the numerical claim to the company's filing and downgraded confidence because the filing covers global revenue, while your question is about U.S. enterprise adoption.
```
